Method · Movement Architecture

Movement architecture.

A pattern is not only a sequence of steps. It is a structure made of timing, direction, connection, pathways, body organization and choices.

Home→Method→Movement Architecture
Explore the MethodTrain in Class
The basic idea

Understand the structure, and you gain more ways to use it.

When dancers memorize only the outer shape of a pattern, they often struggle as soon as the entry, timing, partner response or musical context changes. Movement architecture asks what elements make the movement possible, and which can change without destroying its logic.

Instead of asking…

“What comes next?”

Ask…

“What is organizing this movement right now?”

Six variables

Timing

When does the action begin, continue, suspend or resolve?

Direction

Where is each dancer traveling, and how can that pathway change?

Connection

How is information transmitted, received and maintained between partners?

Body organization

How do weight, axis, rotation, tone and segment relationships support the movement?

Pathway

What route does the body, hand, torso or partnership travel through space?

Decision

Which part is fixed, and where can either dancer adapt based on context?

Why this matters socially

Better adaptation

Adjust to different partners without losing the movement's underlying logic.

Less memorization

Rely less on recalling a long chain and more on recognizing a familiar structure.

More creativity

Variation becomes easier because you know which variables can change.

Faster diagnosis

Ask whether a failure comes from timing, pathway, connection, organization or decision-making.

Transfer

A principle learned in one movement can become useful in another.

Autonomy

Become less dependent on exact demonstrations and more able to solve movement problems independently.

A useful shift

Patterns are examples. Principles are reusable.

How to practice it
1

Learn the movement normally

Get enough familiarity that you can perform the basic version without constant stopping.

2

Name the organizing variables

Identify what matters most: timing, direction, connection, body organization, pathway or decision.

3

Change one variable

Alter timing, entry, exit, direction or partner relationship while keeping the rest stable.

4

Retrieve and vary

Come back to the principle later, then practice it under a slightly different constraint.

5

Return to social dancing

Use the idea with different partners and music to test whether the principle survives outside the drill.
