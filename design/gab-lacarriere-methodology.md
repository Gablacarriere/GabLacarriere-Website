## 1. Purpose of this document

This document is the evolving conceptual source of truth for the **Gab Lacarriere Methodology**.

It exists to prevent the methodology from becoming fragmented across classes, Granola notes, the Atlas, Zoukable, the Mentorship Hub, teacher tools, website pages, conversations, and research projects.

The methodology is broader than the Atlas. The Atlas is the structured curriculum representation of selected student-addressable concepts; this document also includes learning theory, teaching design, cognitive skill, practice architecture, safety, teacher development, and implementation principles.

## 2. Update and provenance rules

The methodology should evolve, but it should not drift casually.

Every meaningful addition should be traceable to one or more of these source types:

1. **Explicit Gab decision** — highest authority for the methodology. A clearly stated methodological decision can become canonical immediately.
2. **Repeated teaching evidence from Granola** — recurring observations can strengthen, modify, or generate candidate principles.
3. **Existing website / tool architecture** — evidence of what has already been formalized or operationalized.
4. **Research evidence** — motor learning, cognitive science, pedagogy, biomechanics, psychology, neuroscience, safety, or related research. Research informs the method; it does not automatically become doctrine.
5. **Chat-fed development** — ideas developed with ChatGPT are candidates unless Gab explicitly adopts them or they are already operationalized.

### Status labels

Use these when a concept is not yet fully settled:

- **Canonical** — adopted part of the methodology.
- **Candidate** — promising, but not yet promoted.
- **Evidence** — observation or research supporting a concept.
- **Open question** — unresolved terminology, relationship, or teaching hypothesis.
- **Deprecated** — previous formulation retained for history but no longer preferred.

### Promotion logic

For curriculum concepts, preserve the existing curriculum-sync rules:

- recurring in at least 3 distinct teaching notes/classes; or
- appearing across at least 2 distinct teaching contexts and clearly generalizing; or
- explicitly identified by Gab as foundational/canonical.

A methodology principle may be adopted sooner when Gab explicitly defines it as part of the method.

### Privacy rule

Granola material is evidence, not publication copy. Never place student names, private quotations, private meeting URLs, or identifiable lesson details in this document or in public website files.

# PART I — THE CENTRAL AIM

## 3. What the methodology is trying to produce

The goal is not to create dancers who can reproduce the largest number of patterns.

The goal is to develop dancers who can:

- perceive what is happening;
- understand the structure of movement;
- organize their own body reliably;
- communicate and receive information through partnership;
- retain and retrieve useful movement knowledge;
- adapt to timing, partner, music, direction, entry, exit, and constraints;
- diagnose problems instead of merely noticing failure;
- make informed choices independently;
- transfer principles from one movement to another;
- continue learning without permanent dependence on a teacher.

**Core proposition:** Teaching should progressively make the student more capable of learning, adapting, and choosing.

**Core educational outcome:** autonomy through transferable understanding and embodied skill.

---

## 4. Foundational pedagogical principles

### 4.1 Diagnosis before correction

A visible error is not necessarily the cause of the problem. The teacher first asks what system is failing: perception, timing, weight transfer, organization, pathway, connection, attention, decision-making, confidence, physical availability, or another constraint.

Correction should target the bottleneck rather than the most visible symptom.

### 4.2 Principles over pattern accumulation

Patterns are examples. Principles are reusable.

Named movements matter, but they should increasingly be understood as combinations, transformations, and applications of reusable mechanisms.

### 4.3 Structure creates freedom

Freedom is not the absence of structure. Reliable structure gives the dancer more meaningful choices.

Understanding what must remain stable makes it possible to identify what can vary.

### 4.4 Transfer over immediate performance

A movement working once in a drill does not establish learning.

Learning becomes more convincing when the skill survives delay, retrieval, new partners, altered timing, different entries/exits, musical changes, or other relevant variation.

### 4.5 Autonomy as the long-term criterion

The teacher should gradually make themselves less necessary.

Advanced learning includes the ability to observe, diagnose, test, correct, and adapt without requiring the exact original demonstration.

### 4.6 Mechanism before ornament

Styling, complexity, speed, head movement, musical variation, or pattern extension should not compensate for an unreliable foundation.

When a more basic mechanism is unstable, reduce complexity until the causal structure can be trained clearly.

# PART II — THE TWO LEARNING LOOPS

## 5. Public learning cycle: UNDERSTAND → FEEL → PRACTICE → ADAPT

This is the primary student-facing learning cycle currently used on the website.

### 5.1 Understand

Build a useful mental model of the movement or problem. Identify what organizes the task rather than memorizing only an external shape.

### 5.2 Feel

Translate the idea into sensorimotor information: weight, timing, direction, tone, pressure, connection, joint organization, momentum, rhythm, and internal sensation.

### 5.3 Practice

Use focused repetitions, constraints, retrieval, feedback, and appropriately scaled difficulty to change the targeted skill.

### 5.4 Adapt

Vary relevant conditions until the skill survives outside the original drill or demonstration.

This cycle describes **how a skill is learned and generalized over time**.

---

## 6. Cognitive-skill loop: RECOGNIZE → VISUALIZE → CALCULATE → CHOOSE → EXECUTE → UPDATE

This is a second, more moment-to-moment layer connecting dance expertise with concepts also useful in chess expertise.

It describes **how a dancer perceives, predicts, decides, acts, and learns while movement is unfolding**.

### 6.1 RECOGNIZE — Temporal Pattern Recognition

**Definition:** the ability to recognize a familiar movement organization while it is unfolding and identify the current phase within its temporal structure.

A dance pattern is not only spatial. It is spatiotemporal: structure, trajectory, rhythm, duration, momentum, connection state, partner orientation, and likely continuations all matter.

**Key idea:** Pattern recognition tells the dancer **where they are**.

### 6.2 VISUALIZE — Sensorimotor Visualization / Motor Simulation

**Definition:** the ability to internally simulate an upcoming movement or sequence, including one’s own body, the partner’s likely response, pathways, weight transfer, momentum, connection changes, rhythm, and duration.

“Visualization” is the student-friendly term. “Sensorimotor simulation” is the more precise methodological term.

**Key idea:** Visualization allows the dancer to imagine **where they could go**.

### 6.3 CALCULATE — Temporal Calculation

**Definition:** the ability to project one or more plausible continuations through time and anticipate the consequences of timing, direction, momentum, partner response, and available movement options.

Calculation in dance is not numerical arithmetic. It is temporal projection under movement constraints.

**Key idea:** Calculation estimates **what is likely to happen next**.

### 6.4 CHOOSE — Contextual Decision

**Definition:** the ability to select an action from plausible options based on intention, safety, partner information, music, space, skill availability, and likely consequences.

Expertise is not only having more options. It includes selecting useful options quickly and suppressing inappropriate ones.

**Key idea:** Choice determines **which continuation is worth executing**.

### 6.5 EXECUTE — Sensorimotor Execution

**Definition:** organizing the selected action through timing, weight transfer, direction, tone, connection, body pathway, coordination, and appropriate force.

A correct conceptual choice can still fail if execution is mistimed, overforced, unstable, or poorly coordinated.

**Key idea:** Execution turns the selected solution into **actual movement**.

### 6.6 UPDATE — Error-Based Model Updating

**Definition:** comparing the expected result with the actual result and modifying perception, prediction, technique, or decision rules accordingly.

The result of one action becomes information for the next action.

**Key idea:** Updating makes every attempt part of **future expertise**.

### Relationship between the two loops

**UNDERSTAND → FEEL → PRACTICE → ADAPT** describes long-horizon skill acquisition.

**RECOGNIZE → VISUALIZE → CALCULATE → CHOOSE → EXECUTE → UPDATE** describes short-horizon perception and decision-making inside the dance.

The methodology should train both.

# PART III — MOVEMENT ARCHITECTURE

## 7. Movement Architecture

A pattern is not merely a sequence of steps. It is an organized relationship among movement variables.

The current public model uses six variables:

1. **Timing** — when an action begins, continues, suspends, changes, or resolves.
2. **Direction** — where the dancer or partnership travels and how orientation changes.
3. **Connection** — how information is transmitted, received, modulated, and maintained.
4. **Body organization** — weight, axis, tone, joints, rotation, posture, segment relationships, and support.
5. **Pathway** — the route traveled by the body, hand, torso, head, or partnership.
6. **Decision** — what is fixed, what is optional, and where adaptation or choice is possible.

### 7.1 Movement as relationships, not isolated shapes

The same visible shape can emerge from different organization. Conversely, the same underlying mechanism can generate different visible forms.

Teaching therefore asks not only **what does it look like?** but **what makes it possible?**

### 7.2 Pattern decomposition and recombination

Named patterns can be analyzed into smaller movement units, transitions, orientations, timing structures, and partner relationships.

This supports:

- diagnosis;
- transfer;
- creativity;
- recombination;
- recognition of pattern families;
- reduced dependence on memorized chains.

### 7.3 Constraint and affordance logic

Partner position, blocks, contact, direction, momentum, timing, and available space alter the set of possible continuations.

Teaching should help dancers perceive these possibilities rather than only reproduce predetermined routes.

# PART IV — ATLAS / CURRICULUM ARCHITECTURE

## 8. Atlas design principles

The Atlas organizes curriculum using two independent dimensions:

- **Radius = curriculum depth**, not student rank.
- **Angular neighborhood = functional / conceptual similarity.**

Relationships represent support, explanation, or stabilization rather than rigid prerequisites.

### Depth bands

1. **Core** — high-reuse ideas organizing many later actions.
2. **Foundations** — recurrent mechanisms built on the core.
3. **Integration** — combinations of foundations in partner and movement tasks.
4. **Application** — named patterns, off-axis work, and context-specific use.
5. **Exploration** — advanced transformations and analytical lenses.

### Current eight domains

1. **Body Organization & Weight**
2. **Frame & Connection**
3. **Rhythm & Timing**
4. **Space & Orientation**
5. **Movement Grammar**
6. **Head Movement & Off-Axis**
7. **Torsion & Body Pathways**
8. **Learning & Practice**

### Foundational placement principle

The most reusable, high-leverage concepts should tend toward the center. More specialized, advanced, or context-dependent concepts should tend outward.

Conceptual neighbors should be meaningfully related. Spatial proximity is itself pedagogical information.

# PART V — PRACTICE ARCHITECTURE

## 9. Deliberate practice principles

### 9.1 One primary focus at a time

A repetition is easier to learn from when one question is foregrounded.

Other skills still operate, but the learner should know what variable is currently being tested.

### 9.2 Retrieval before re-exposure

Immediate reproduction is not durable recall.

When appropriate, ask the learner to reconstruct a concept or movement before rewatching, rereading, or being shown the answer again.

### 9.3 Spacing

Prefer repeated encounters distributed across time rather than relying only on massed repetition in one session.

Zoukable may operationalize this with spaced-repetition logic, but physical fatigue, tissue loading, complexity, and error quality must remain part of scheduling decisions.

### 9.4 Variability with control

Variation should test transfer without changing so many variables that the learner cannot identify what caused success or failure.

Useful variations can include:

- side;
- tempo;
- music;
- partner;
- entry;
- exit;
- direction;
- embrace;
- timing family;
- range of motion;
- degree of assistance;
- environmental constraint.

### 9.5 Layering after reliability

Additional layers are introduced when the base action can continue with enough automaticity that attention can move elsewhere without collapse.

### 9.6 Errors as information

Errors should be used diagnostically. The aim is not merely to avoid errors but to identify what they reveal about the learner’s current model, perception, coordination, or constraints.

### 9.7 Practice should produce transfer

The end point of a drill is not a good drill performance. The end point is improved performance in the target environment: social dance, improvisation, class recall, competition, choreography, or another intended context.

# PART VI — KINESTHETIC AND SENSORIMOTOR LEARNING

## 10. Kinesthetic practice

Explanation is useful until explanation is no longer the primary bottleneck.

When the learner already understands the task conceptually, additional talking may have diminishing value. The intervention can shift toward physical exposure, sensation, repetition, contrast, constraints, and feedback.

### 10.1 Sensory discrimination

Learners should become better at distinguishing relevant sensations rather than simply being told whether a repetition is correct.

Possible contrasts include:

- stable vs collapsed support;
- informative vs excessive pressure;
- complete vs incomplete weight transfer;
- local isolation vs traveling undulation;
- active vs passive initiation;
- stiff vs adaptable frame;
- continuous vs interrupted pulse.

### 10.2 External and internal information

Teaching may use visual, auditory, tactile, proprioceptive, vestibular, rhythmic, and conceptual information. The useful mix depends on the current bottleneck.

### 10.3 Sensorimotor simulation

Mental rehearsal can be used to anticipate timing, weight, pathways, partner response, and connection before execution, especially when linked to the cognitive loop described above.

# PART VII — PARTNERING LOGIC

## 11. Partnership as information exchange

Partner dance is not one person issuing a fixed command and the other reproducing a memorized answer.

The partnership continuously exchanges information through contact, position, timing, movement, momentum, available space, and adaptation.

### 11.1 Leading as proposal

The leader organizes information, constraints, opportunities, and timing strongly enough for a continuation to become available without relying on force.

### 11.2 Following as active interpretation

The follower remains responsive to current information rather than automatically completing the movement they predicted.

Pattern recognition supports orientation; it should not replace listening.

### 11.3 Frame as adaptable organization

Frame is not a fixed arm shape. It is the organization of the body around contact so information can remain clear while the partnership changes direction, tone, position, or proximity.

### 11.4 Connection as channel

Connection is the usable exchange of information between partners. It can migrate between contact points and should not require maximal pressure everywhere.

# PART VIII — RHYTHM, TIMING, AND TEMPORAL ORGANIZATION

## 12. Pulse before variation

A stable internal pulse is foundational. Timing variation should preserve temporal orientation rather than become uncontrolled speeding up or slowing down.

### 12.1 Timing as choice

Timing families are deliberate organizations of action within musical time, not merely faster or slower versions of the same pattern.

### 12.2 Continuity through transitions

Counting, pulse, and weight transfer continue through pauses, transitions, and apparent pattern endings.

### 12.3 Phrasing and energy

Musical structure includes phrase, density, pause, accent, introduction, resolution, and changes of energy. Movement can adapt without abandoning structural organization.

### 12.4 Lambada temporal logic

Lambada may organize proposals and responses across rhythmic blocks using question–answer relationships. Style-specific temporal structures should remain distinct when the evidence supports real differences.

# PART IX — SAFETY, PREPARATION, AND PHYSICAL READINESS

## 13. Safety as a design constraint

Safety is not a separate disclaimer added after technique. It is part of movement architecture, progression, range selection, partner responsibility, and teaching design.

### 13.1 Progressive demand

Higher-demand movement should be built on adequate support, control, range, coordination, and partner information.

### 13.2 Head movement and off-axis work

Head movement should be understood as a whole-body pathway rather than an isolated neck action.

Off-axis tasks should build from reliable weight transfer, torsion, lateral organization, shared information, and progressively scaled demand.

### 13.3 Warm-up as part of teaching design

Warm-up contains two distinct layers:

**General warm-up** — raises overall readiness and moves/warms the body broadly.

**Specific warm-up** — prepares the exact skills, ranges, coordination, tissues, movement patterns, and partner demands that will be used in the class.

Specific warm-up can include simplified versions or prerequisite drills for the class movement.

### 13.4 Cool-down

Cool-down is a deliberately designed section rather than leftover time. Its role can include down-regulating intensity, restoring comfortable movement, transitioning out of demanding ranges or repeated patterns, and consolidating key takeaways when appropriate.

# PART X — TEACHING DESIGN

## 14. Class architecture

A class should be designed around learning goals and prerequisite relationships rather than only around a sequence of figures.

A useful class-design process asks:

1. What is the target capability?
2. What prerequisite knowledge or physical availability does it require?
3. What is likely to be the main bottleneck for this group?
4. What general and specific warm-up prepares the task?
5. Which explanation or demonstration is necessary?
6. Which drills isolate the important mechanism?
7. How will difficulty progress?
8. How will students retrieve or reconstruct rather than merely copy?
9. How will the skill be reintegrated into partner work, music, or social use?
10. What evidence would show that learning transferred?
11. What cool-down or closing reflection is appropriate?

### 14.1 Practice density

Teaching time should contain enough actionable practice. Long blocks should not be padded with explanation when the learning task itself is simple.

### 14.2 Scaffolding and progression

Complexity can be manipulated through range, speed, number of variables, partner responsibility, predictability, timing, support, and contextual demands.

### 14.3 Differentiation

Students can work on the same underlying concept with different constraints or progression levels.

### 14.4 Teacher judgment remains active

Automated curriculum tools can propose structures, but they do not replace observation, context, adaptation, and teacher responsibility.

# PART XI — FEEDBACK AND DIAGNOSIS

## 15. Feedback as model correction

Feedback should improve the learner’s ability to detect and correct relevant differences, not create permanent dependence on teacher evaluation.

### 15.1 Feedback questions

Useful diagnosis can ask:

- What did the learner intend?
- What actually happened?
- What did they perceive?
- Which variable changed unexpectedly?
- Was the problem conceptual, perceptual, temporal, physical, relational, or decisional?
- What is the smallest useful intervention?

### 15.2 Feedback quantity

More corrections are not necessarily better. Prioritize high-leverage information and allow enough repetition for the learner to test the change.

### 15.3 Updating loop

Feedback should eventually feed the learner’s own **UPDATE** process: prediction → execution → comparison → model revision.

# PART XII — LONGITUDINAL LEARNING SYSTEMS

## 16. Mentorship methodology

The Mentorship Hub / Mothership implements the method over time rather than only within one class.

It should combine:

- goals;
- curriculum/Atlas relationships;
- teacher judgment;
- student preferences and constraints;
- personalized learning roadmaps;
- check-ins and longitudinal trend detection;
- practice assignments;
- session history;
- links into drills and Zoukable where appropriate.

The roadmap is not a rigid syllabus. It is a visible hypothesis about useful next learning steps that can be revised as the student changes.

## 17. Check-in logic

Progress should not be represented by one opaque score.

The mentorship system can track multiple dimensions, including:

- Progress;
- Support / Belonging;
- Challenge Fit;
- Goal Alignment;
- Engagement / Usefulness;
- Overall Experience.

These dimensions help the teacher detect problems that technical performance alone may miss.

# PART XIII — ZOUKABLE / PRACTICE SYSTEM

## 18. Drills as training units

Zoukable should distinguish curriculum concepts from drills.

A drill can train one or more concepts without itself becoming a concept node.

Useful drill metadata can include:

- primary and secondary objectives;
- linked skills/concepts;
- solo / partner / either;
- leader / follower relevance;
- tempo and rhythm;
- minimum and recommended duration;
- left/right tracking where relevant;
- corrective purpose;
- progression level;
- confidence or mastery evidence;
- prerequisites;
- physical constraints or adaptations.

## 19. Spaced practice without punishment

Spaced repetition can help revisit skills, but a physical learning system should not copy vocabulary-flashcard logic blindly.

Mistakes should generate information and useful re-exposure rather than erase progress. Teacher overrides and diagnostic drills remain important.

# PART XIV — TEACHER DEVELOPMENT

## 20. Teaching as its own discipline

Being able to dance does not automatically imply being able to teach dance.

Teacher expertise includes:

- observation;
- diagnosis;
- decomposition;
- sequencing;
- explanation;
- demonstration;
- task design;
- feedback;
- differentiation;
- safety;
- practice design;
- assessment;
- curriculum design;
- reflection and revision.

### 20.1 Teacher-facing tools

The methodology should support at least two distinct planning levels:

**Curriculum Creator** — long-horizon planning: domains, dependencies, sequence, coverage, progression, and coherence.

**Class Creator** — lesson-level planning: goals, prerequisites, warm-up, specific preparation, explanations, drills, progressions, integration, assessment, and cool-down.

Warm-up and cool-down should be explicit components rather than optional afterthoughts.

# PART XV — RESEARCH AND EVIDENCE STANDARD

## 21. Evidence-informed, not evidence-decorated

Scientific language should not be added merely to make teaching sound authoritative.

When research is used, distinguish:

- direct evidence in dance;
- evidence from broader motor learning or sport;
- plausible analogy;
- teaching hypothesis;
- Gab’s practice-based observation.

Claims should be updated when stronger evidence becomes available.

### 21.1 Methodology vs scientific claim

A teaching principle may be useful and canonical as part of the method even when direct experimental evidence in Brazilian Zouk or Lambada is limited. In that case, describe it accurately as a methodological principle rather than presenting it as experimentally proven.

The intended flow is:

**NEW INPUT → CLASSIFY → COMPARE → DECIDE → UPDATE → PROPAGATE**

Possible inputs include:

- Granola class/private notes;

- new website/tool functionality;

- methodology principle;

- pattern family;

Check whether it:

- already exists;
